# Italian Genre Playbook

Starting points for Italian repertoire. Same shape as `genre-playbook.md` — style-box template, working BPM, slider bands — but for the traditions Suno handles differently when the lyric is in Italian.

Templates are starting points, not formulas. A template used verbatim produces a template-sounding song; the vocal block is the first thing to replace.

Pair every entry with `italian-language.md`. Genre gets you the arrangement; pronunciation gets you a record a native listener believes.

## Cantautorato

### Cantautorato classico (1965–1980)
`70–100 BPM` · W `0.15–0.35` · SI `0.60–0.80`
```
Italian male baritone, conversational near-spoken phrasing, consonant-forward,
unpolished, sung in Italian with native diction. Acoustic singer-songwriter,
nylon-string guitar, upright piano, double bass, restrained string section.
84 BPM, warm analog mix, natural room, vocal upfront and dry.
```
The defining trait is that the voice is *not* beautiful — it's legible. Ask for polish and you lose the tradition. `unpolished`, `dry`, `close-mic`, `no reverb wash` do more here than any genre tag.

Lyric density runs high: long lines, narrative, few repeats. Stay inside 6–12 syllables per line anyway, and let the chorus be a refrain rather than a hook.

### Cantautorato moderno / indie d'autore
`80–110 BPM` · W `0.25–0.45` · SI `0.55–0.75`
```
Italian male tenor, flat affect, deadpan delivery, slightly nasal, close-mic,
sung in Italian with native diction. Indie pop, jangly clean guitar, warm analog
synth, live-feel drums, simple bass. 96 BPM, tape saturation, roomy mix.
```

### Itpop / indie italiano contemporaneo
`100–120 BPM` · W `0.25–0.45` · SI `0.55–0.75`
```
Italian male vocal, unaffected and slightly flat, conversational, doubled on the
hook, sung in Italian. Italian indie pop, bright clean guitar, analog synth pad,
programmed drums with live feel, melodic bass. 112 BPM, glossy but warm,
vocal wide and upfront.
```
The vocal understatement is the genre. `no belting, no vibrato` in the exclusions keeps Suno from turning it into radio pop.

## Pop and mainstream

### Pop italiano radiofonico
`100–125 BPM` · W `0.10–0.30` · SI `0.65–0.85`
```
Italian female alto, open vowels, melodic pop phrasing, clean modern delivery,
layered harmonies on the hook, sung in Italian with native diction. Italian pop,
punchy programmed drums, piano, bright synth, sub bass. 116 BPM, radio-ready
polish, wide stereo, vocal upfront.
```

### Sanremo / musica leggera orchestrale
`70–110 BPM` · W `0.10–0.30` · SI `0.65–0.85`
```
Italian tenor, theatrical vibrato, sustained belt on the hook, orchestral
phrasing, sung in Italian with native diction. Orchestral pop ballad, full
string section, grand piano, brass swells, timpani. 78 BPM, wide hall reverb,
big dynamic build into the final chorus.
```
This is one of the few Italian forms where Suno's instinct toward grandeur is an asset. Structure matters: the genre expects a modulation and a bigger final chorus — cue it with `[Chorus - final, key change, maximum]`.

### Leggera anni Sessanta
`90–130 BPM` · W `0.15–0.35` · SI `0.60–0.80`
```
Italian male baritone, warm crooning, wide vibrato, sung in Italian. 1960s
Italian orchestral pop, big band brass, lush strings, acoustic guitar, upright
bass, brushed drums. 118 BPM, mono-leaning vintage mix, plate reverb,
period recording character.
```

## Hip-hop and trap

### Rap italiano boom bap
`85–95 BPM` · W `0.20–0.40` · SI `0.55–0.75`
```
Italian male rap flow, clipped consonants, conversational cadence, dry vocal,
rapped in Italian. Boom bap, sampled jazz drums, dusty upright bass, filtered
Rhodes loop, vinyl crackle. 90 BPM, underground aesthetic, vocal centred and dry.
```
Use `[Rap Verse]` rather than `[Verse]` — a plain `[Verse]` tag pulls the delivery toward singing. Italian rap tolerates a higher word count than sung Italian; up to ~500 words is workable.

### Trap italiana
`130–150 BPM half-time` · W `0.25–0.45` · SI `0.55–0.75`
```
Italian male vocal, auto-tuned, melodic ad-libs, laid-back behind the beat,
sung and rapped in Italian. Dark trap, 808 bass with long glides, crisp hi-hat
rolls, sparse minor-key keys. 140 BPM half-time feel, atmospheric,
heavy low end.
```

```
Italian male rap flow, aggressive clipped delivery, Northern Italian diction,
```

## Electronic and dance

### Italo disco
`115–135 BPM` · W `0.30–0.55` · SI `0.50–0.70`
```
Male vocal, earnest and melodramatic, accented delivery, heavy reverb.
Italo disco, analog synth lead, arpeggiated sequenced bassline, LinnDrum
pattern, gated snare, string machine pads. 124 BPM, glossy plastic sheen,
wide stereo, bright 1980s production.
```
Historically italo was sung in accented English by Italian singers — if that's the effect you want, write the lyric in English and keep `accented delivery`. For Italian lyrics, add the language anchors and expect a slightly different, more modern result.

### Italo house / cosmic
`118–126 BPM` · W `0.35–0.55` · SI `0.45–0.65`
```
Italo house, piano stabs, deep filtered bassline, organ chords, congas,
soulful wordless female vocal sample. 122 BPM, warm analog mix, long builds,
spacious dub delays.
```
*(Largely instrumental — drop the vocal block or keep it to a wordless line.)*

### Eurodance italiana anni 90
`135–145 BPM` · W `0.20–0.40` · SI `0.65–0.85`
```
Female vocal, powerful belted hook, plus male spoken-rap verses. Italian
eurodance, supersaw synth stabs, four-on-the-floor kick, rolling bassline,
piano riff. 140 BPM, bright compressed mix, huge reverb on the hook.
```
The male-rap / female-sung split is structural, not decorative. Alternate the section tags per voice and state `dual vocalists, male rap verses, female sung chorus` in the style box.

## Napoli

### Canzone napoletana classica
`60–90 BPM` · W `0.15–0.35` · SI `0.65–0.85`
```
Neapolitan tenor, operatic vibrato, melismatic, full chest voice, sung in
Neapolitan with native diction. Classic canzone napoletana, mandolin tremolo,
nylon-string guitar, accordion, lush strings. 72 BPM, warm vintage mix,
natural hall, vocal front and centre.
```
`mandolin tremolo` is the single most identifying instrument tag — name the technique, not just the instrument.

### Neomelodico
`80–105 BPM` · W `0.15–0.35` · SI `0.60–0.80`
```
Neapolitan male tenor, heavy vibrato, emotional belt, melismatic runs, sung in
Neapolitan. Neomelodico, synth strings, programmed drums, electric piano,
mandolin accents. 92 BPM, glossy modern mix, vocal loud and compressed,
big reverb.
```
The production is deliberately over-bright and the vocal deliberately over-compressed. Asking for tasteful mixing here defeats the genre.

## Folk and tradition

### Pizzica / taranta
`140–180 BPM` · W `0.20–0.40` · SI `0.65–0.85`
```
Female vocal, raw open-throated folk delivery, ornamented, sung in Salentino
dialect. Pizzica salentina, frame drum tamburello driving 12/8, diatonic
accordion, violin, nylon-string guitar. 160 BPM, live acoustic recording,
natural room, communal energy.
```
The `tamburello` in a fast compound metre is the genre. If Suno gives you a straight 4/4, the rhythmic tag isn't landing — restate it as `driving 12/8 tambourine ostinato` and lower Weirdness.

### Liscio romagnolo

```
Italian male vocal, warm and cheerful, straightforward phrasing, sung in
Italian. Liscio ballroom, lead diatonic accordion, clarinet, brushed drums,
walking upright bass, in waltz and polka rhythms. 140 BPM, clean live-band
recording, dance-hall ambience.
```

### Cantopopolare / canzone di protesta

```
Mixed group vocals in unison, plain unadorned delivery, sung in Italian.
Italian folk protest song, acoustic guitar, accordion, snare drum march
```

## Italian artists: describe, never name

Suno filters real artist and band names — the prompt fails or quietly degrades. The pattern is always the same: **decompose the artist into genre + vocal texture + instrumentation + production era.** The decomposition is usually more useful to Suno than the name would have been.

The table gives the sonic content, not a value judgement about who resembles whom. Adapt it: the point is the method.

| Instead of naming… | Write |
|---|---|
| the Genoese narrative cantautore | `Italian baritone, near-spoken narrative delivery, cantautorato, nylon-string guitar, chamber strings, dry vintage mix` |
| the Bolognese poet-songwriter | `Italian tenor, warm and theatrical, wide leaps, cantautorato with jazz harmony, piano, clarinet, lush strings` |
| the Milanese pop-rock innovator | `Italian male vocal, urgent and slightly strained, 1970s Italian pop rock, layered acoustic and electric guitar, Moog, ambitious arrangement` |
| the Sicilian avant-pop philosopher | `Italian tenor, declamatory and detached, art pop with synth and orchestra, minimalist repetition, operatic quotation, cold precise mix` |
| the great female interpreter of the leggera | `Italian mezzo-soprano, enormous range, dramatic belt with fine control, 1960s orchestral pop, big band and strings` |
| the Roman rock everyman | `Italian male vocal, hoarse and raw, conversational shouted delivery, Italian rock, distorted guitars, Hammond, stadium production` |
| the Neapolitan blues fusion guitarist | `Neapolitan male vocal, husky and soulful, Mediterranean blues fusion, nylon and electric guitar, saxophone, Latin percussion` |
| the Roman glam rock export | `Italian male vocal, snarling androgynous delivery, glam rock revival, fuzzy riff guitar, driving bass, raw modern-retro production` |
| the itpop indie deadpan | `Italian male vocal, flat affect, deadpan, Italian indie pop, jangly guitar, analog synth, warm lo-fi production` |
| the Milanese conscious rapper | `Italian male rap flow, dense internal rhyme, dark reflective boom bap, filtered soul sample, live bass, cinematic strings` |
| the Milanese trap pioneer | `Italian male vocal, heavily auto-tuned, melodic ad-libs, dark trap, 808 glides, sparse minor keys` |
| the Roman prog quartet | `Italian progressive rock, Hammond and Mellotron, complex time changes, operatic tenor, long instrumental development` |
| the spaghetti western composer | `spaghetti western score, whistled melody, twanging reverb guitar, wordless choir, ocarina, huge desert reverb` |
| the Neapolitan tenor tradition | `Neapolitan tenor, operatic vibrato, melismatic, mandolin tremolo, lush strings, warm vintage recording` |

Ordinary Italian words can also collide with a producer tag and break a generation. If a prompt fails with no obvious cause, change the spelling of the most unusual word in it before assuming anything else.

## Where Suno struggles with Italian material

Honest limits, so you don't burn credits on the wrong fix.

- **Dialect** is the weakest area by far. Neapolitan gets closest because it's the best represented; Salentino, Sardinian and Friulian are largely guesswork. Respell aggressively and keep dialect in its own sections.
- **Tamburello and traditional compound metres** flatten toward 4/4. Lower Weirdness and restate the rhythm as an ostinato.
- **Mandolin** frequently arrives as a generic plucked string. `mandolin tremolo` survives better than `mandolin`.
- **Accordion** drifts toward a French musette colour. `diatonic accordion, Italian folk phrasing` pulls it back.
- **Long cantautorato lines** get compressed. This is the standard lyric-length problem, but it bites harder here because the tradition is wordy — keep the total under ~3,000 characters and cut a verse rather than shortening every line.
- **Final vowels** are a pronunciation problem, not a genre problem. Everything about them is in `italian-language.md`.
